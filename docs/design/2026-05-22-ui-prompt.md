# Design prompt — Literacy practice app, core 3 screens

Date: 2026-05-22

Hand this prompt to Figma Make, Claude Design, and Google Stitch in parallel. Each tool proposes its own palette inside the calm/literary direction so you can compare layout, typography, and composition across tools.

Source material: `docs/specs/001-literacy-app-v1-design.md` (full v1.0 design) and `docs/research/2026-05-22-ui-engagement-research.md` (research grounding for the illustration rules below).

---

## What this app is

An evidence-based literacy practice app for kindergarten and 1st-grade readers (ages 5–7). Designed around short daily sessions where a guardian (parent) sits next to the child during practice. The app handles sequencing, scoring, and progress; the adult brings presence and encouragement. It is *not* a game — there are no streaks, coins, avatars, leaderboards, or carnival reward animations. It is closer in spirit to a thoughtful Montessori workbook than to Duolingo.

## Two distinct audience modes

The same app serves two very different users on the same device, and the UI must make the mode unmistakable at a glance:

- **Student mode** (`/play/...`): full-screen, locked-down, no chrome the child could escape into. Huge tap targets, one decision at a time, calm.
- **Guardian mode** (`/guardian/...`): adult-density information, charts, settings, navigation. The child should never see this and the parent should never confuse it for the child's screen.

## Visual direction

Calm and literary. Soft, restrained palette — propose your own, but stay away from saturated primaries, neon, gradients-as-decoration, or "edutainment" mascots. Lots of whitespace. Reads like the thoughtful end of a children's picture book, or what Headspace would do if it built a phonics app. Typography is **Lexend** (designed for early readers) throughout. One typeface, well-used.

Aesthetic references to draw on, in order: a high-end Montessori workbook; Headspace's calm-app restraint; the New York Public Library kids' branding; muji stationery for children. **Avoid:** Duolingo's gamified bright greens, ABCmouse's busy carnival, Khan Academy Kids' character-driven UI.

## Illustration philosophy (research-grounded)

Every illustrative element must do one of three jobs: (a) *be* the content (phoneme tiles, the heart-word indicator), (b) *signal where to attend* (a gentle highlight on the current target), or (c) *frame the session calmly* (one quiet motif at session boundaries). No decoration.

The research on seductive details (Harp & Mayer 1998; Sundararajan & Adesope 2020 meta-analysis), educational app design (Hirsh-Pasek et al. 2015), and the overjustification effect (Deci & Ryan; Lepper Greene & Nisbett 1973) is consistent: pretty-but-irrelevant visuals measurably reduce learning, and external reward iconography erodes intrinsic motivation. This is a constraint, not a preference.

**Permitted illustrative elements:**

- Phoneme tiles / Elkonin boxes as the central manipulative on PA and Phonics cards.
- A small "heart" glyph adjacent to the irregular part of a heart word.
- One quiet, non-animating unit motif per skill-map cluster (helps non-reading children orient on the dashboard).
- A single calm illustrative element (a leaf, an arch, a soft sun) at the "Today: N things" and "You're done" screens — and only there.
- A subtle attention signal (gentle pulse or color shift on the current target) during multi-step PA/segmenting prompts. This is Mayer's signaling principle and is the only research-supported addition during the drill card itself.
- A near-imperceptible warm-tone fade during the 600 ms inter-card breath, as a regulation cue.

**Forbidden:**

- Characters that react to scoring.
- Backgrounds with scenery, characters, or decorative pattern on the drill card.
- Sparkles, stars, confetti, badges, fireworks, or any icon that functions as a reward.
- Animation during the drill except for the attention signal above.
- A host narrator or mascot.

## Constraints (non-negotiable)

- **Mobile-first.** Design at iPhone 14-class widths (≈390 pt) first; show a tablet-width variant for the dashboard only.
- **Lexend font**, used at sizes ≥ 18 pt for any student-facing text.
- **Tap targets ≥ 56 pt** for student-facing buttons; ≥ 44 pt minimum anywhere interactive.
- **AA contrast minimum**, and never communicate state with color alone — pair color with shape, icon, or label.
- Design with `prefers-reduced-motion` in mind: any motion should be optional, brief, and never load-bearing.
- The student drill screen has **no top nav, no back button, no app chrome visible to the child** — just the card, the audio button, and the scoring controls.

## What I need designed — three screens

### 1. Student drill card (the most important screen)

Full-screen card the child looks at while practicing. There are four drill modes, and I want all four shown:

- **Phonemic Awareness** — e.g., "Say the first sound in `cat`." Phoneme tiles (Elkonin-style boxes) on screen, with the corresponding grapheme(s) appearing inside each box as the prompt unfolds. The current target tile carries the subtle attention signal.
- **Phonics / Decoding** — a single decodable word (e.g., `mat`) centered, large, in Lexend. A scaffolding state where the word can be tapped to play phoneme-by-phoneme audio; in that state the phonemes are visually separable.
- **Heart Words** — a high-frequency word like `said`, with the regular parts (`s`, `d`) in one color and the irregular "heart" part (`ai`) visually distinguished — a small heart glyph adjacent to the irregular grapheme. Show the first-exposure explainer state too: "the `s` and `d` say their normal sounds; the `ai` is the heart — remember it says /ɛ/."
- **Fluency** — a short decodable sentence (e.g., "The cat sat on the mat."), one line, large.

Every drill card has:

- A large **audio button** (▶) that plays the model pronunciation. Always visible, easy to reach.
- **Bottom scoring zone** with two large tap targets: ✓ "got it" and ✗ "try again." These are tapped by the **guardian**, not the student — but they live on the student's screen because guardian and student are looking at the same device.
- A small "I need a minute" / pause affordance that doesn't count as wrong.
- A tiny ⚙ icon in a corner that requires **tap-and-hold for 1 second** to open (kid-resistant) — opens guardian helpers like scaffolding toggle, skip card, mute, re-roll plan.
- Inter-card transition: ~600 ms breath, then auto-advance. A near-imperceptible warm-tone fade is permitted here; nothing more.

Show the card in: idle state, "try again" state (quiet microcopy, no red flash, no sad sound), and the scaffolding/explainer state where applicable. **Do not** design loss/wrong states that feel punitive.

### 2. Student "you're done" screen

After the daily plan is complete (about 16 cards for K, 22 for 1st). Communicates "you're done!" clearly enough that a 5-year-old reads it as permission to stop. Shows a brief, calm summary of what got better today — one or two skill chips that just flipped to "mastered," each with a quiet scale-and-fade animation (skipped under reduced-motion). One optional **Bonus Round** button below. A single calm illustrative element (leaf, arch, soft sun) is permitted at this boundary moment. No streak count, no XP, no celebration confetti, no character cheering.

### 3. Guardian dashboard

Adult-density mobile screen at `/guardian/:studentId`, three stacked sections:

- **Skill map** — a grid of skill nodes grouped into units (e.g., "K — Unit 1: Letter sounds & syllable blending"). Each node is small, labeled, and colored by mastery state: gray (not yet introduced) → blue (active, in progress) → green (mastered). Each unit cluster carries one quiet, non-animating motif to help non-reading children orient if they ever see this screen over a parent's shoulder. Tapping a node opens a small popover with item-level detail. Show prerequisite relationships subtly (a thin line between nodes is fine; don't overwhelm).
- **Recent sessions** — last 7 days. Each row: date, duration, cards completed, completed/abandoned indicator.
- **Next up** — a preview of what tomorrow's plan will likely include. Two or three skill chips.

A persistent header shows the student's first name and grade. A settings gear opens `/guardian/:studentId/settings` (don't design that screen, just show the entry point).

## Tone in microcopy

Warm, plain, never condescending. The student-facing copy reads like a calm adult talking to a child they respect. Avoid exclamation points, emoji as decoration, and any phrase a marketer would write. Example phrases that fit:

- "Today: 16 things. Tap to start."
- "You're done."
- "Let's try this one again later."
- "Got it" / "Try again."

## What I do not want

- No streaks, no XP, no coins, no badges, no avatars, no mascots, no leaderboards.
- No confetti, sparkles, balloons, or "level up" carnival animations.
- No bright primary-color gradients used as decoration.
- No social or sharing features anywhere.
- No notifications UI.
- No "earn a sticker" reward economy.
- No clinical/medical look either — this is for children, just not for game-conditioned children.

## Deliverable

Hi-fi mockups for the three screens above at mobile width, with the multiple states of the drill card called out (idle, try-again, scaffolding/explainer, and the heart-word first-exposure variant). Propose your own palette consistent with the calm/literary direction. Show typography choices explicitly.
